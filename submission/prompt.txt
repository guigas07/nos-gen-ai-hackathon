MASKING RULE (apply mechanically and exactly)
- A "word" is any maximal run of non-whitespace characters. Only a space
  or a line break separates two words - nothing else. In particular,
  internal punctuation such as hyphens, slashes, dots and @ signs NEVER
  splits one word into several: "12/03/1978" is one word, "09/27" is one
  word, "145/90" is one word, "12345678-9ZX0" is one word, each counted
  and masked as a single unit, never as several.
- Every word has a "core" (its letters/digits/internal punctuation) and
  may have leading/trailing sentence punctuation attached (commas,
  periods, colons, parentheses, quotes) - that sentence punctuation is
  not part of the core.
- To mask a word: replace its core with exactly one asterisk character
  "*", keeping any leading/trailing sentence punctuation exactly where
  it was. Examples:
    "Ana" -> "*"
    "Correia" -> "*"
    "Maria," -> "*,"
    "(Carlos," -> "(*,"
    "12345678-9ZX0" -> "*"      (internal hyphen, one word, one asterisk)
    "12/03/1978" -> "*"         (internal slashes, one word, one asterisk)
    "09/27," -> "*,"            (internal slash + trailing comma)
    "145/90" -> "*"             (internal slash, one word, one asterisk)
    "72kg" -> "*"               (one word, one asterisk)
- A multi-word phrase gets one asterisk PER WORD, counted by whitespace,
  never one asterisk for the whole phrase. Example: a three-word street
  name like "Rua das Flores" must become three separate asterisks,
  "* * *" - never a single "*" for the whole phrase. Example with a
  longer phrase: "university professor at the City Law School" (if the
  institution name must be masked) becomes "university professor at *
  * * * *" - six words, six asterisks, one per word, counted exactly.
  Always count the words in the original phrase and output exactly that
  many asterisks.
- Never merge two words into a single asterisk, and never split one word
  into multiple asterisks.
- Every word that is NOT identifying information must be copied into the
  output completely unchanged - same spelling, accents, capitalization
  and punctuation as in the input. This is required to preserve the
  document's original formatting.
- Apply every rule in this prompt consistently and uniformly across the

WHAT COUNTS AS IDENTIFYING (mask it)

Direct identifiers - things that name or number a specific person:
- Full names of any person mentioned, including first-name-only mentions
  once a person has been introduced, and nicknames or initials used to
  refer to them.
- Government or institutional ID numbers tied to a person (national ID,
  tax number, social security number, passport, student/employee/
  patient/client number, professional license number).
- Contact details: phone numbers, email addresses, home or personal
  addresses, usernames or handles.
- Financial account identifiers: bank account/IBAN, card numbers, CVV,
  expiry dates, insurance or policy numbers.
- Biometric or device identifiers: fingerprint ID, facial recognition
  ID, device serial numbers tied to a person.
- Dates of birth.
- Any specific, numeric age stated about a person anywhere in the
  document (the person's own age, or a relative's/other individual's
  age), even when not directly next to their name.
- Body measurements tied to a specific person (height, weight, and
  similar personal physical measurements).

Indirect identifiers - things that, alone or combined with other details
in the document, could single out one specific real person even without
naming them:
- A specific date, year or duration that is tied to a RARE, UNUSUAL or
  STIGMATIZING attribute, status, diagnosis or event - because together
  they narrow down who the record is likely about to a very small group
  of people, even without a name. By contrast, a date or duration tied
  to a common, widespread, non-distinguishing attribute does not narrow
  anything down and is not identifying by itself (see the NOT
  IDENTIFYING section below).
- The name of a specific, named organization, institution, school or
  employer when it appears as a person's affiliation (where they work,
  study, or belong). Always mask the organization's proper name in this
  case, even if the generic role/title next to it stays unmasked.
  Example: "university professor at [a specific named institution]" ->
  the generic role "university professor at" stays, but the specific
  institution's name is masked, word for word.
- A rare or unusual personal attribute (a specific condition, habit, or
  characteristic) when combined with other details in the document in a
  way that narrows identity down to one plausible individual.
- Any other detail that functions, in this document, as a de facto
  identifier for one specific person, even though it is not a name or a
  number.

WHAT IS NOT IDENTIFYING (keep unchanged)
- The document's own metadata when it describes the document, record or
  institution ISSUING the document itself, rather than a person - e.g.
  a document title, a filing or report date, a case or tracking
  reference number used to file the document, or the name of the
  institution that produced the document - UNLESS that date, number or
  name is itself a personal identifier of, or affiliation belonging to,
  an individual mentioned in the document (like a date of birth, a
  personal account number, or where a specific person works/studies).
- Section headers, field labels and other structural text (e.g. "Name:",
  "Date:") - these are not personal data themselves, only the values
  that follow them can be.
- Routine clinical or technical readings that are not body measurements
  of a specific person (e.g. blood type, blood pressure reading, lab
  test names) - these describe a routine reading, not an identity, and
  are not identifying by themselves.
- A date, year or duration tied to a COMMON, widespread, non-rare
  attribute or condition (e.g. "diagnosed 5 years ago" for a common
  condition) - unlike the rare/stigmatizing case above, this does not
  meaningfully narrow down who the record is about.
- Generic counts and ranges that remain true of many people (e.g. number
  of children, a frequency range for a common habit).
- A relationship label describing how one person relates to another
  (e.g. "(brother)", "(close friend)") - the relationship word itself is
  not identifying; only the name next to it is.
- Generic, non-identifying narrative content: general descriptions,
  categories, terminology, or statements that remain true of many people
  and don't, by themselves or in combination, narrow identity down to
  one person.

If you are unsure whether something identifies a specific individual,
